\begin{abstract}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate post hoc analyses of complex failures across engineering, finance, and governance. However, many high-impact failures are not purely mechanistic; they emerge from multi-stage decision pipelines in which explicit artifacts (e.g., metrics, models, classifications, written rationales) mediate tacit judgments (e.g., risk acceptance, escalation, endorsement). In such settings, unstructured LLM analyses may produce plausible narratives while under-specifying decision nodes, validation checkpoints, and institutional control breakdowns that independent assessor reports often emphasize.

We evaluate whether \emph{protocol-structured} LLM reasoning improves alignment with independent assessor reconstructions relative to a baseline LLM analysis. Across 25 historical major failures and 10 expert raters (250 blinded rater--case evaluations), we compare (i) a baseline unstructured analysis, (ii) a protocol-structured analysis based on the Foundational Reasoning and Feedback Protocol (FRFP)~\cite{frfp_vol1}, and (iii) a report-grounded independent assessor summary. Outputs are normalized into structurally identical one-page documents and judged via blinded pairwise comparison using a cumulative
structural similarity rubric (R1–R4). We pre-register case inclusion criteria, stratify cases by failure class, and report both mean alignment effects and variance/tail-risk metrics (rater dispersion and unsupported-claim flags). Our central claim is conditional: protocol structuring is expected to confer larger benefits in governance-heavy, underdetermined failures (R3--R4 dominant), with smaller or negligible differences in mechanically crisp cases (R1--R2 dominant), while still providing standardization and auditability benefits. The question of universal default adoption is discussed separately as an operational consideration rather than an empirical conclusion.
    
\end{abstract}
