% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{This paper investigates conditions under which AI systems in high-stakes scientific and regulatory workflows can be governed and trusted using only automated checks, and when human tacit judgment remains structurally necessary. We introduce FRFP, a categorical protocol model that separates explicit artefacts (prompts, tools, reports) from tacit states (understanding, judgment), with a single boundary and a small set of epistemic primitives, and show that this “kernel” is initial/canonical among architectures satisfying our explicit–tacit axioms. Building on this, we develop a dynamic model with finite tacit capacity, degradation, and requirement maps, and analyze stochastic pipelines via hazard profiles and safe-horizon functionals. Under the FRFP assumptions, we prove conditional inevitability theorems: along sufficiently long runs, some accepted output will fall below its grounding requirement, and no explicit-only detector, oracle, or governance operator can guarantee the absence of hallucination—i.e., can guarantee correctness across all admissible pipelines and populations. We extend the framework to collective and institutional settings and derive structural non-automation results for governance. We close with protocol templates, case sketches, and FRFP-compatible instruction sets that demonstrate how the formal architecture, given the stated assumptions on explicit–tacit separation and degradation, constrains the design of realistic human–AI workflows.}

\section{Introduction}
\begin{comment}
AI-based reasoning systems are increasingly used as partners in scientific work: they propose conjectures, sketch proofs, draft experiments, and synthesise literature. A single project can now involve months of back-and-forth with a collection of such tools: conjectures refined, drafts rewritten, code patched, experiments reprioritized. This creates both opportunities and risks. These systems can operate over large explicit structures that are difficult for humans to inspect directly, but they can also generate plausible yet invalid or insufficiently grounded content, sometimes with high confidence. In most current workflows, humans and AI systems interact in an untyped, informal way. Inputs, outputs, and corrections share a single channel; correctness judgments are not explicitly separated from exploratory or heuristic suggestions; and long interaction traces (a sequence of human–AI messages; see Section~2 for a formal definition) ...
 often suffer from semantic drift. This makes it difficult to reason formally about correctness, responsibility, and long-run behaviour.

We understand the scientific method as the organised process of proposing explicit hypotheses or models, deriving predictions, gathering empirical data, and revising or rejecting those hypotheses in light of the evidence. Over time, the scientific community has developed institutional practices that help stabilise scientific claims: repeatable procedures, reproducible results, and organised peer review of conclusions. Our FRFP protocol is designed to represent these patterns in a unified way, by separating explicit artefacts\footnote{By \emph{explicit} we mean artefacts that can be represented as finite, machine-manipulable symbolic structures (text, code, programs, datasets, proofs, etc.) without loss of their correctness-relevant content. In the formal development these form the explicit space \(E\).} (hypotheses, models, data, analyses) from tacit judgments\footnote{By \emph{tacit} we mean human-exclusive evaluative states—understanding, background know-how, and context-dependent judgment—that bear correctness authority and are not treated, in FRFP, as fully captured by any fixed finite symbolic encoding. In the formal development these form the tacit space \(T\).} about their adequacy, credibility, and acceptance.

In this paper, we introduce a protocol-level design for human--AI collaboration, the \textbf{Foundational Reasoning and Feedback Protocol (FRFP)}, with three aims:
\begin{itemize}
  \item to provide a formal semantics for mixed human--AI reasoning trajectories;
  \item to make explicit the boundary between what AI systems may and may not do in scientific workflows;
\item to enable theorem-level analysis of correctness and long-run behaviour (for example, conditions under which certain kinds of hallucination cannot be ruled out).\footnote{Throughout, such ``inevitability'' claims are made only within the FRFP formalism and under the assumptions set out in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, not as universal statements about all possible AI systems.}
\end{itemize}
\end{comment}
\noindent
AI-based reasoning systems are increasingly used as partners at work across domains from science to business: they draft, critique, plan, and propose. A single project can now involve months of back-and-forth with multiple such tools---drafts rewritten, code patched, analyses rerun, experiments reprioritised, decisions revisited---creating both opportunities and risks. These systems can operate over large explicit structures that humans cannot easily inspect, yet they can also produce plausible but invalid or weakly grounded content, sometimes with high confidence.  Complex systems are routinely built and operationalised from imperfect components by adding redundant layers of safety checks and escalation paths\cite{nrc_defense_in_depth_glossary,iaea_insag10_defence_in_depth_1996,reason_human_error_models_management_2000,reason_latent_human_failures_complex_systems_1990}. The same engineering fact suggests a protocol view of human--AI collaboration: reliability should be an emergent property of the workflow, not an assumption about any single component.

\smallskip
\noindent
The core issue is not occasional error but \textbf{unstabilised collaboration}: we increasingly depend on long, multi-step human--AI workflows whose intermediate artefacts, assumptions, and evaluation criteria shift over time. In many settings this drift occurs inside one conversational channel, with no stable separation between exploration and acceptance and no durable checkpoints where correctness and responsibility are explicitly reasserted. As a result, most workflows remain untyped and informal: inputs, outputs, and corrections share a channel; correctness judgments are not separated from heuristic suggestions; and long interaction traces (a sequence of human--AI messages; see Section~2 for a formal definition) often suffer semantic drift---making correctness, responsibility, and long-run behaviour hard to analyse \cite{laban_llms_get_lost_multiturn_2025,dongre_drift_no_more_context_equilibria_2025,arike_evaluating_goal_drift_agents_2025,choi_identity_drift_llm_agents_2024}.

\smallskip
\noindent
We take the scientific method as organising inspiration because its stabilisation norms---replication, reproducibility, and peer review---are historically established and widely standardised, providing a canonical template for converting exploratory work into accepted claims.\footnote{We use ``scientific'' for workflows whose primary aim is stable, publicly checkable claims about the world, stabilised by intersubjective norms such as replication, reproducibility, and peer review. By contrast, much non-scientific work aims at action under objectives and constraints (e.g., safety, cost, policy, profit), where acceptance is stabilised by organisational controls (tests, audits, approvals, monitoring) rather than truth-claims alone. The same explicit/tacit separation applies in both: what changes is the form of ``evidence'' and the institutional mechanism of acceptance.}
We abstract from that template a \textbf{domain-general} protocol for human--AI collaboration: in non-scientific settings (business, policy, engineering), ``evidence'' and ``review'' differ in form, but the same long-horizon drift and accountability problems arise. Most examples in this paper are non-scientific.

\smallskip
\noindent
In this paper, we introduce a protocol-level design for human--AI collaboration, the \emph{Foundational Reasoning and Feedback Protocol} (FRFP), with three aims:
\begin{itemize}
  \item to provide a formal semantics for mixed human--AI reasoning trajectories;
  \item to make explicit the boundary between what AI systems may and may not do in such workflows;
  \item to enable theorem-level analysis of correctness and long-run behaviour, including conditions under which certain kinds of hallucination cannot be ruled out.\footnote{Throughout, such ``inevitability'' claims are made only within the FRFP formalism and under the assumptions set out in Section~\ref{sec:epistemicdynamics}, not as universal statements about all possible AI systems.}
\end{itemize}

\noindent
FRFP’s central design choice is a sharp separation between two modes of operation: an \emph{explicit} mode of machine-manipulable representations and pipelines, and a \emph{tacit} mode of human-exclusive evaluative, correctness-bearing states. AI systems are confined to explicit mode; only humans inhabit tacit mode. A single review-and-belief-update boundary carries explicit artefacts into tacit evaluation, with no direct channel in the opposite direction. The rest of the paper formalises this architecture and studies long-horizon human--AI workflows within it.

\smallskip
\noindent
Informally, FRFP is a human--AI conversational calculus: a typed calculus of interaction traces in which AI systems make only explicit moves, humans retain tacit correctness-bearing moves, and entire workflows admit compositional semantics and dynamic analysis.

\paragraph{Scope of novelty.}
We rely on standard categorical constructions---free categories, groupoids, fibrations, Grothendieck constructions---but assemble them in service of explicit/tacit separation and feedback in human--AI workflows. The novelty lies in:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item isolating a small set of epistemic primitives (explicit/tacit separation, boundary, feedback);
  \item combining them into a compositional architecture in which system-level properties (e.g., auditability, correctness preservation, and conditional inevitability results for certain failure modes such as hallucination) can be formulated and proved as theorems inside the FRFP assumptions; and
  \item working at a level of abstraction that allows later work to reuse existing categorical theory rather than inventing bespoke formalisms for each application domain.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope of novelty.}
We rely on standard categorical constructions---free categories, groupoids, fibrations, and Grothendieck constructions---but assemble them as follows to formalise explicit/tacit separation and feedback in human--AI workflows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a minimal set of epistemic primitives (explicit/tacit separation, boundary, feedback);
  \item a compositional architecture in which system-level properties---auditability, correctness preservation, and conditional inevitability results for failure modes such as hallucination---are stated and proved as theorems under FRFP assumptions; and
  \item an abstraction level that lets later work reuse existing categorical theory rather than introducing bespoke formalisms per application domain.
\end{enumerate}

Our use of FRFP in this paper is threefold: we prove theorems internal to the model; we conjecture that its structural assumptions approximate many high-stakes workflows in practice; and, conditional on that empirical premise, we derive design implications for institutional oversight.

\subsection{FRFP architecture overview}
\label{sec:frfp-architecture-overview}
We organise FRFP into five epistemic layers:

\textbf{Interaction Protocols.}
Rules governing human--AI interaction: who may say what, in which modes (explicit vs.\ meta-level), and in which order. This layer constrains admissible traces but does not yet assign meaning.

\textbf{Epistemic Spaces.}
Admissible traces are interpreted over two state spaces.\footnote{We use ``epistemic'' in the standard sense: pertaining to knowledge, belief, or correctness-bearing state. In FRFP, epistemic structures track correctness-relevant information (what is written down, computed, or logged, and what is tacitly accepted), as distinct from surface interaction structure.}
We distinguish: (i) an explicit space of knowledge-like artefacts (what is written down, computed, or logged); and (ii) a tacit space of correctness-bearing states (what humans accept or are prepared to stand behind). A one-way boundary maps explicit artefacts into tacit evaluation.

\textbf{Epistemic Algebra.}
This layer specifies the allowed \emph{operations} on explicit workflows---how we decompose tasks into steps, compose steps into pipelines, navigate between subproblems, and refactor a workflow without changing its intent. These operations act only on explicit artefacts. At designated review points, the one-way boundary carries the current explicit artefact(s) into tacit evaluation, producing a human judgment update. The result is an ``algebra'' of explicit workflows equipped with a tacit correctness semantics: we can reason about how workflow transformations affect what a human ends up accepting.

In this volume we keep the tacit side intentionally minimal. We treat the tacit space $T$ and its operators as a correctness-bearing interface: a black-box human layer that (i) receives explicit artefacts only via the one-way boundary and (ii) exposes only a finite, observable set of judgments. The technical structure therefore lives on the explicit side. Detailed models of individual cognition or institutional psychology are out of scope and deferred.


\textbf{Epistemic Dynamics.}
The evolution of knowledge- and correctness-bearing state over time and under load: tacit degradation, requirement schedules, hallucination as requirement violation, and safe horizons for running a workflow.

\textbf{Collective Epistemic Dynamics.}
Knowledge and correctness in populations and institutions: multiple agents sharing explicit artefacts, communicating via conversational workflows, and operating under policies, committees, and governance operators. This layer captures collective dynamics and the limits of explicit-only consensus and governance.

\textbf{Running example.}
Throughout the paper we illustrate these layers with a simple case: a human uses an AI assistant to choose a ``best'' airfare, where best is not cheapest. The assistant may explore and structure options, but the accept/reject judgment remains with the human.

\subsection{Contributions}

Formally and conceptually, this volume makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Explicit--tacit epistemic architecture.}
  We propose FRFP as an explicit--tacit architecture for long-horizon human--AI workflows, based on an explicit/tacit separation and a one-way review boundary.

  \item \textbf{Category-theoretic realisation and semantics.}
  We realise this architecture category-theoretically: an algebra of explicit pipelines and a semantics mapping whole workflows to tacit correctness judgments. In the model, correctness is invariant under admissible explicit transformations and navigation, so system-level properties can be stated and proved at the pipeline level rather than per step.

  \item \textbf{Dynamics of tacit context and hallucination.}
  We develop a qualitative long-horizon dynamic model: tacit capacity is finite and degrades under load; requirements evolve; and feedback from explicit artefacts shapes future judgments. Under these assumptions we prove conditional inevitability results: over sufficiently long admissible runs, failure modes including hallucination (accepting artefacts that do not meet active requirements) cannot be ruled out, and safe horizons are necessarily finite even when explicit semantics are fixed.

  \item \textbf{Collective and stochastic settings.}
  We extend the analysis from single users to populations and from deterministic to stochastic implementations. We model collective executions as processes over populations of tacit states, analyse how consensus interacts with explicit--tacit separation, and show that stochasticity determines which failure trajectory is realised rather than precluding failure, yielding notions such as hazard profiles and safe-horizon functionals.

  \item \textbf{Limits of explicit-only mechanisms and non-automation.}
  From explicit--tacit separation and human-exclusive grounding of correctness, we derive conditional impossibility results for purely explicit mechanisms. Under FRFP assumptions, no explicit-only detector or governance operator can guarantee the absence of hallucination or correctness failure across all admissible pipelines and populations. This yields a structural non-automation claim: in domains fitting the FRFP architecture, tacit human oversight is necessary at the governance layer.

  \item \textbf{Disciplined agents and FRFP-compatible tooling.}
  We introduce \emph{disciplined agents}: AI tools and surrounding infrastructure confined to the explicit side of FRFP, exposing reasoning in pipeline form and integrating with tacit oversight rather than replacing it. We state requirements (e.g., source--reason traceability, navigation integrity, explicit confinement) and outline supporting tooling (run-keepers, context managers, competence monitors).

  \item \textbf{Protocols, case studies, and design patterns.}
  We translate the formal architecture into protocol-level design principles for long-horizon human--AI collaboration. We state foundational rules for FRFP-compatible practice (explicit confinement, human-exclusive grounding, source--reason traceability, integrity under navigation), illustrate them in case studies (e.g., literature review, financial scenario analysis), and give examples of FRFP-aligned system instructions and prompting patterns for explicit-only tools. These patterns show how FRFP can constrain interfaces, workflows, and institutional arrangements in high-stakes settings.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related Work}

Our work sits at the intersection of formal semantics, human--AI interaction, AI safety governance, and social epistemology. We position FRFP against mechanised reasoning, categorical semantics and rewriting, HCI-style human--AI frameworks, governance and evaluation protocols, and formal models of scientific communities, and then relate it to the hallucination and calibration literature.

\subsubsection{Interactive proof, type theory, and mechanised reasoning.}
Proof assistants and type-theoretic systems (e.g., Coq, Isabelle, Lean) distinguish proof objects from proof scripts and provide soundness guarantees for derivations within a fixed formal calculus. Users supply goals and proof terms; the central question is whether a term inhabits a type or a proof object checks a specification. FRFP is orthogonal: we do not propose a new logic or proof theory, and correctness is not a syntactic property of explicit objects. Instead, correctness is a tacit attribute of human epistemic state, accessed only via the explicit--tacit boundary, and the target is long-horizon workflows in which correctness cannot be reduced to proof checking or type inference.

Within a fixed formal system, some correctness properties (e.g., that a proof term checks) are decidable and verifiable entirely in explicit space. In FRFP terms, provers and test harnesses act as explicit correctness engines: morphisms internal to $E$ that certify properties of explicit artefacts. Our non-automation results address a broader, tacit notion of correctness for real-world workflows and institutions, which cannot be discharged by any explicit-only oracle. Such tools are therefore best viewed as tacit-insensitive inner layers: once a calculus and specification are tacitly fixed, inner-task correctness becomes explicit, while tacit judgment continues to govern the choice, adequacy, and revision of the formalism itself (Appendix~D.4).

\subsubsection{Category theory and categorical semantics.}
The categorical tools we use---free categories, groupoids, (op)fibrations, Grothendieck constructions---are standard in categorical semantics of languages and logics, where they model syntax/semantics maps, type systems, effects, and compositional program behaviour. Our contribution is to repurpose these tools for epistemic roles in mixed human--AI reasoning: explicit and tacit subcategories linked by a one-way boundary, navigation capturing refactoring and exploration, and a combined workflow category in which whole runs are single compositional objects. This supports theorem-level statements of auditability, correctness invariance, and explicit-only impossibility results as properties of a unified architecture rather than informal desiderata.

\subsubsection{Rewriting, confluence, and canonical forms.}
Our explicit reduction systems and confluence arguments draw on standard abstract reduction systems and termination/confluence techniques from term rewriting. The novelty is not the rewriting machinery but its epistemic role: normalisation shows that admissible explicit refactorings and navigation steps collapse to canonical forms that induce the same tacit correctness judgments. Rewriting thus yields invariance of tacit acceptance under the exploratory restructuring typical of long-horizon human--AI pipelines.

\subsubsection{HCI-style human--AI interaction and appropriate reliance.}
HCI and human--AI interaction research studies task allocation, communication, and co-adaptation, often organised along dimensions such as locus of control, initiative, transparency, and feedback. Recent surveys and experiments analyse trust, distrust, and \emph{appropriate reliance}, including how explanations and interface choices affect over- and under-reliance \cite{Eckhardt2024Reliance,Schemmer2023Appropriate,Visser2025Trust}. These approaches typically treat the boundary at the level of tasks and interfaces, with correctness and responsibility mediated by constructs such as calibrated assistance. FRFP is compatible in spirit but imposes a sharper architectural constraint: correctness is, by construction, human-exclusive. AI-mediated operations remain in explicit space; systems cannot access tacit state as input or cross the explicit--tacit boundary; and interaction patterns such as suggestion and critique are treated as morphisms in a formal workflow semantics. This yields invariance and impossibility results at the class-of-interactions level and provides an architectural envelope within which HCI design choices must sit.

\subsubsection{AI safety governance, risk committees, and evaluation protocols.}
AI safety and governance literatures emphasise organisational and regulatory mechanisms: safety frameworks, red-teaming, incident response, internal risk committees, standardised evaluation suites, and secure development practices, often organised as multi-line-of-defence models. Recent syntheses, such as METR's \emph{Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies}, extract shared components of lab policies for capability thresholds, evaluations, deployment safeguards, and governance structures \cite{METRCommonElements}. These proposals typically treat correctness or hallucination as system properties measured by evaluations (e.g., benchmark failure rates) and assume that appropriately designed protocols deliver acceptably low failure.

FRFP differs in three respects. First, evaluations, committees, and governance operators are explicit constructions atop the workflow architecture; questions such as whether explicit-only consensus can recover correctness, or whether a fully automated governance oracle can exist, become formal questions about morphisms and functors. Second, explicit--tacit separation yields explicit-only impossibility results: under FRFP assumptions, broad classes of detectors, consensus mechanisms, and governance operators cannot, in principle, recover tacit correctness or eliminate hallucination across all admissible pipelines and populations. Third, FRFP introduces dynamic safe horizons: rather than focusing only on static capability scores, we analyse degradation, requirement schedules, and hazard profiles for whole workflows, shifting attention from benchmark scores to how long a given protocol can be run under specified controls before risk exceeds a tolerable threshold.

\subsubsection{Epistemic models of scientific communities.}
Formal social epistemology and philosophy-of-science models represent communities as stylised agents that select research options, exchange evidence or beliefs, and update via simple rules. Work on \emph{network epistemology} studies how communication structure and incentives affect convergence, replication success, and the division of cognitive labour \cite{Zollman2013Network,Grim2013ScientificNetworks}. These models share FRFP's interest in individual versus collective performance and institutional structure, but typically work with explicit belief states (e.g., probability vectors or epistemic landscapes) and rarely separate explicit representations from tacit state. FRFP instead builds explicit/tacit modality into the foundation: explicit objects are manipulable by AI systems and institutions, while tacit states remain human and correctness-bearing. This underwrites results showing that certain institutional arrangements cannot reconstruct correctness from explicit data alone.

\subsubsection{Hallucination, calibration, and uncertainty in language models.}
Work on hallucination, calibration, and uncertainty surveys failure modes and benchmarks, develops semantic-uncertainty methods, and studies when models can assess their own correctness and why standard training pipelines yield hallucinations and miscalibration \cite{Zhang2023Siren,Huang2025HallucinationSurvey}. Hallucination is typically treated as mismatch between outputs and a ground-truth distribution or benchmark; the focus is on architectures, training objectives, or decoding strategies that reduce or detect such errors. FRFP instead treats hallucination as a protocol-level property: a mismatch between tacit context and active requirements at the moment an explicit artefact is accepted. The definition is model-agnostic but institution-dependent, since requirement maps encode domain- and institution-specific standards. Model-side detectors can reduce short-run error by filtering artefacts before acceptance, but so long as tacit state degrades and nontrivial requirements recur, our dynamic analysis implies that sufficiently long admissible runs will eventually include requirement-violating acceptances. Because detectors operate entirely in explicit space, they cannot arrest long-run degradation of tacit grounding. FRFP is therefore complementary to model-level hallucination and calibration work, but targets protocol and governance design and the limits of explicit-only guarantees.

\subsubsection{Human--AI collaboration and protocol design.}
There is a growing literature on AI support for discovery, literature review, hypothesis generation, experimental design, and broader knowledge work, typically organised around specific tasks, phases, or interface patterns. Much of this work optimises local performance (speed, coverage, quality) and studies usability constructs (transparency, control, reliance), but leaves long-horizon questions---semantic drift across extended traces, responsibility for acceptance, and the limits of automation---only implicitly specified. FRFP provides a protocol-level frame: long-horizon workflows are treated as compositions in an explicit workflow architecture with a fixed boundary to tacit space. Within this frame, ``copilots'', risk committees, and red-team evaluations become concrete patterns of morphisms and institutions. FRFP's distinctive combination is an explicit/tacit semantic architecture with human-exclusive correctness, a dynamic theory of tacit degradation and protocol-level hallucination with inevitability results, and an institutional layer yielding structural non-automation and explicit-only impossibility results for governance mechanisms.
